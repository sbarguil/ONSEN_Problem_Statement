ONSEN Problem Statement

Abstract

The IETF has published a number of YANG data models for automating the provisioning and delivery of network and connectivity services, including the L2SM, L3SM, L2NM, L3NM, Attachment Circuits, and Network Slicing models. These models are intended to provided abstracted representations of common operator services, exposing only the necessary parameters for configuring and managing services. These abstractions help to contain the complexity of service provisioning within the necessary domains, making the implementation of complex, multi-domain services easier to implement and manage. However, operators have reported persistent challenges in operationalizing these abstractions in a consistent, scalable, and automatable manner. This document describes the problem space for the ONSEN Working Group, identifying the operational gaps and deficiencies in existing IETF service and network abstraction models that complicate, or prevent effective end-to-end automation. The problems documented here are initially drawn from operator experience, and from the findings of the IAB NEMOPS Workshop. It is a living draft, not intended for publication as an RFC, and will be updated with new problem descriptions as the ONSEN work proceeds. This document does not propose solutions, protocols, or new data models.¶

1. Introduction

The IETF has published several YANG data models that are intended for automating the provisioning and management of service provider connectivity services. These include models such as L3SM [RFC8299], L3NM [RFC9182], L2SM [RFC8466], L2NM [RFC9291] and Service Attachment Points (SAPs) [RFC9408]. A new YANG model for the Network Slice Service [RFC9543], is currently being authored: [I-D.ietf-teas-ietf-network-slice-nbi-yang].¶

While some of these abstractions have been deployed, operators report persistent challenges in operationalizing them. As highlighted by the IAB NEMOPS Workshop [NEMOPS], these challenges are systemic and operational in nature. They are not confined to a specific technology or service type, but reoccur across abstraction domains and deployment environments.¶

In addition, despite the availability of numerous YANG data models - covering configuration, assurance, and fault management - and the ongoing effort to make these models coexist within a common framework under the IETF umbrella, operators continue to face significant challenges in operationalizing YANG-based service APIs in a consistent, scalable, and interoperable manner. While models such as the L3SM, L2SM, L3NM, L2NM, AC/SAP abstractions and Network Slice Service each address specific aspects of service delivery, it is not always clear which models should be used together, in which scenarios, or to what extent a given implementation actually supports the full model. The usage of these APIs remains fragmented - often partially implemented - and difficult to automate end-to-end. In practice, APIs generated from similar YANG models often differ in service semantics, and the lack of clear guidance on model composition and interoperability complicates integration across systems, vendors, and deployment environments.¶

The Operationalizing Network and SErvice abstractioNs (ONSEN) Working Group is chartered to address this problem space by focusing on the operational aspects of network and service abstractions. It aims to make it easier to implement and use the IETF's service and network abstractions, with the goal of improving network automation, operational efficiency, and interoperability.¶

This document defines the problem space for ONSEN. It does not propose solutions, protocols, or new data models.¶

2. Conventions and Definitions

The following terms are used in this document:¶

AC:

Attachment Circuit¶

Abstraction:

The process of defining simplified, high-level constructs that represent network and service-level capabilities, while hiding the details of their underlying realization. Abstraction enables interaction between management and automation systems without requiring direct exposure of device-specific configurations or protocol behaviors.¶

BSS:

Business Support System¶

LxNM:

Layer x Network Model (L2NM or L3NM)¶

LxSM:

Layer x Service Model (L2SM or L3SM)¶

NEMOPS:

Next Era of Network Management Operations¶

ONSEN:

Operationalizing Network and SErvice abstractioNs¶

OSS:

Operation Support System¶

SLO:

Service Level Objective¶

Service Model Intent:

The desired service behavior expressed by an operator through a service model (LxSM), as described in [RFC8309]. This refers to the set of customer requirements — connectivity, performance, availability — submitted to the orchestration layer and expected to be maintained by the network over time. When this document uses the term "service intent", it refers to Service Model Intent in this sense.¶

3. Structural LxNM/LxSM Problems

The following sections highlight structural problems observed in the current LxNM and LxSM models, which impact their usability and composability in real-world deployments.¶

3.1. Difficulty in Selecting and Composing Service Models

Operators report persistent difficulty in selecting the relevant service model(s) for a given deployment scenario, and in applying them in real-world deployments. The L2SM [RFC8466], L3SM [RFC8299], ACaaS [RFC9834], and NSS [I-D.ietf-teas-ietf-network-slice-nbi-yang] all define service abstractions at a similar level in adjacent domains. Insufficient guidance exists on how to effectively use and compose these service models in practice. No implementation guidance information exists on if, when, and how they should, or should not be combined.¶

3.2. Inconsistent Parameter Availability and Naming

Very similar, if not identical, features and functionality across different network and service models often use slightly different parameters names, YANG data types, or do not provide the same level of configuration detail.¶

4. Limited Observability and Service Assurance

Existing service and network models focus on configuration and provide no standardised mechanism for reporting whether a service is being delivered as ordered. This gap applies both to discrete operational state — whether the service is up and fault-free at a given moment — and to historical SLO compliance — whether the service has met its contracted performance targets over a given period.¶

SAIN ([RFC9417], [RFC9418]) addresses observability internally within the provider domain through an assurance graph that enables operators to troubleshoot outages. It is not designed to be exposed to customers or to the service model layer. SAIN can contribute through a feedback loop from the SAIN Controller to the Service Orchestrator, which would then populate relevant state in LxSM/LxNM; however, that interworking is not currently defined.¶

[RFC9375] provides raw performance monitoring data but does not include a model for expressing the outcome of evaluating that data against the SLOs set in the service model.¶

The gap is a customer-facing service delivery reporting layer that does not currently exist in LxSM or LxNM.¶

5. OSS/BSS Interface and API Interoperability

YANG data models are commonly used as the basis for APIs that expose service abstractions to external systems. However, existing work provides limited guidance on how these abstractions should be exposed, versioned, or consumed in a predictable and interoperable manner.¶

5.1. Northbound API Semantics

Orchestration and controller systems have widely adopted YANG as a schema language, but a lack of equivalent standards for API semantics on orchestrator northbound APIs has resulted in divergent implementations and interoperability challenges.¶

Standardized semantics for service creation, modification, and decommissioning would simplify the implementation of orchestrators and controllers, and enable better interoperability across vendors and deployments.¶

5.2. TMF API-Based Semantics

Some operators use [TMF640]/[TMF641] as the northbound API for service ordering from their BSS. There is no specification of how these interfaces can be aligned with YANG service and network models. Operators must either pay commercial OSS/BSS vendors to build bespoke interfaces or build and maintain their own adaptation layer. The absence of a defined API creates integration complexity, lack of re-useability, vendor lock-in risk, and inconsistent implementations across deployments.¶

6. Insufficient Guidance for Implementing YANG-based Orchestration

With the YANG Data Store [RFC7950] and the NMDA architecture [RFC8342] the IETF has standardized a clear operational model for exposing configuration and operational state from an individual NETCONF/RESTCONF server.¶

YANG has since gained widespread adoption as a schema language for describing the data model at varous abstraction layers, above the network devices themselves. In other words, YANG is increasingly used to define the schemas for OSS systems, orchestrators, and controllers. A prime example are the IETF LxSM and LxNM models.¶

A variety of Informational RFCs, including The YANG Module Classification [RFC8199], Service Models Explained [RFC8309], and A Framework for Automating Service and Network Management with YANG [RFC8969] have advanced the classification of, and relationships between YANG modules as well as the software components that implement them.¶

Operators consistently report they require more precise guidelines, and a much more concrete service stack. This includes a requirement for clear guidelines on how and when to combine IETF standardized models with vendor models and user models at each layer. Further guidance is needed on the interactions between the Service and Network Models. Previous IETF work has attempted to define an exact mapping between these layers, which was determined to be impractical, yet guidance on how to manage these interactions is still needed.¶

6.1. Other Southbound Management Interfaces

Operators depend on a heterogeneous mix of network devices, and network controllers, some of which are YANG model-driven, and others that are not. Vendor-specific APIs, and legacy management mechanisms such as CLI and SNMP remain prevalent. A mix of these interfaces is common even within a single deployment.¶

Operators require a standardized approach to integrating non-YANG modeled devices into YANG-model driven orchestration systems. This includes the ability to apply a YANG-modeled intent to non-NETCONF/RESTCONF devices, and to collect telemetry data from non-YANG modeled devices in a way that can be correlated with YANG modeled service state and performance data.¶

7. Missing LxSM and LxNM Functionality

Operators who use the LxSM and LxNM models in real-world deployments report that the models do not provide the constructs needed to express the full range of service behaviors and operational requirements.¶

7.1. On-Demand and Scheduled Service Operations

Existing YANG service and network models are primarily designed for static, long-lived services. They do not provide the constructs needed to express service behaviors that vary over time, are triggered on demand, or follow a defined schedule. As operators introduce services such as data-intensive workload transmission or SD-WAN-like dynamic VPN capabilities, this gap becomes a concrete operational blocker.¶

[RFC9922] defines a generic model for scheduling that could be bound to LxSM service instances. ONSEN can leverage this model to define how dynamic scheduling can be applied to the LxSM service models.¶

8. Items for Future Study

The following problem areas were raised during the IETF126 ONSEN work group meeting and subsequent mailing list exchanges. They are currently sufficiently developed or prioritised for inclusion in the core problem statement. They are documented here for completeness and to solicit community input on whether and how they should be addressed by ONSEN or other working groups.¶

8.1. Quantum-Security Requirements in Service Models

An increasing number of customers require long-term data confidentiality and protection against future quantum-computing threats. Current service models do not provide a way to express quantum-security requirements or capabilities. It is not yet clear whether this is best addressed through extensions to existing models or through new model constructs. This item is considered lower priority relative to the core problems above and may be revisited in a later revision.¶

8.2. Secure Handling of Secrets in Orchestration Workflows

Device-level configuration often requires credentials, authentication keys, or other sensitive data. There is currently no standardised mechanism for referencing sensitive information securely across the orchestration stack and resolving it only at the point of device configuration. Whether this belongs within the scope of ONSEN, NETMOD, or another WG is an open question that requires further discussion.¶

8.3. Generic Abstraction-to-Underlay Mapping

Operators repeatedly encounter the challenge of mapping service-layer abstractions to underlying infrastructure resources in a generic, technology-independent way. Existing work addresses this problem in specific contexts (e.g., TE-based approaches in TEAS) but no broadly applicable mechanism exists. The ONSEN charter defines the device layer as the lowest layer in scope; mapping below the device layer is therefore out of scope. Further information from the community on the exact problem being identified is needed before this item can be evaluated for inclusion.¶

8.4. Absence of Reusable Service Configuration Constructs

The LxSM models do not provide templates or reusable constructs to aid operators in reducing the input parameters required for common site deployment patterns. Operators must manually configure each service instance, which increases the risk of misconfiguration and reduces operational efficiency.¶

YANG Configuration Templates [I-D.tt-netmod-yang-config-templates], being worked on in NETMOD already provide a generic solution that addresses repetitive YANG configuration instances.¶
